Contractor Invoice Template

Labour, materials, staged payments — an invoice template built the way construction and trades actually bill. Free, online, no signup.

Contractor invoicing is different from ordinary billing: jobs run for weeks, materials are bought up front, and nobody pays the full amount in one go. A contractor invoice has to split labour from materials, connect to the original quote or contract, and fit a staged payment schedule — deposit, progress payments, final balance. The sample below is a kitchen remodel billed exactly that way. Use the same structure for any trade — plumbing, electrical, carpentry, renovations — and your invoices will match what homeowners, property managers and insurers expect to see.

What to include on a contractor invoice

  • Your business name, licence or registration number, and contact details
  • Client name and the job site address (if different from billing address)
  • Invoice number, issue date and the stage it covers (deposit / progress / final)
  • A reference to the signed quote or contract number
  • Labour itemised by day or task, kept separate from materials
  • Materials with quantities — receipts attached for big-ticket items
  • Change orders, listed separately from the original scope
  • Payments already received, and any retention being held
  • Payment terms and the due date for this stage
  • Warranty or guarantee terms for the workmanship

Why staged billing protects contractors

The fastest way for a contractor to bleed cash is to front weeks of labour and thousands in materials, then invoice once at the end. Staged billing fixes that: a deposit before you start, progress invoices as milestones are hit, and a final invoice on sign-off. This template is built for that rhythm — each stage is its own clean invoice referencing the same job. Keep materials and labour on separate lines and clients (and their insurers) stop disputing totals. Retention is a percentage (often 5–10%) the client holds back until the job is signed off and any snagging is done. Show it as a deduction on the progress invoice and invoice it separately once released.
List change-order work as its own lines, separate from the original scope, and reference the change order the client approved. Never bury extra work inside the original lines — that is where arguments start.
Many contractors add a handling markup (10–20%) to materials to cover sourcing, transport and warranty risk. Whether you show the markup as its own line or fold it into unit prices is a business choice — be consistent.
